
Literature Review
This chapter reviews the literature and technologies foundational to the Smart Campus Second-Hand Trading Platform. It examines the current marketplace gaps, machine learning techniques for item recommendations, and sustainability modeling for carbon tracking. Additionally, it evaluates the development frameworks, specifically Flutter and Node.js, to justify the technical feasibility of the proposed system. These findings establish the necessary framework for the subsequent design and implementation phases.
Research Background
The Smart Campus Second-Hand Trading Platform is proposed to address the growing digital economy and the financial challenges faced by university students in Malaysia. According to the Department of Statistics Malaysia, Malaysia's e-commerce income has grown significantly, showing that Malaysians are very comfortable with online shopping (Department of Statistics Malaysia, 2025). However, large e-commerce platforms often ignore the specific needs of university students. Due to the rising cost of living, many Malaysian students are turning to second-hand trading to save money on their education and daily expenses (Sheridan Mahavera & Salma Fairus, 2025).

Students' learning habits have also changed from using physical books to digital materials. As noted by Hashim, Nasir, and Hassim (2024), the wide use of digital devices has made e-books one of the most popular forms of educational media among university students, reducing the reliance on traditional printed textbooks. Instead, students now prefer to buy and sell electronic accessories, such as second-hand Apple Pencils, keyboards, and laptop stands. Besides physical items, students also share and trade digital study notes or past-year exam papers. However, physical materials are still needed. During exam seasons, photocopy shops on campus are still crowded because students need hardcopy notes for revision. Consequently, this creates a mixed demand for both physical and digital items, a specific niche that is hard to fulfill on general public trading platforms.

Apart from study materials, student housing in Malaysia also creates a need for a campus marketplace. Many students live in partially furnished or unfurnished rental houses outside the campus, which do not have basic furniture like study tables, bed frames, and small appliances. Selling heavy and bulky furniture online is often avoided by individuals due to the significant hassle of packaging and the high cost of courier shipping logistics (Chow, 2025). Therefore, students usually trade hostel essentials like electric kettles and multi-cookers through other ways. The best way to trade these heavy items is through face-to-face meetups or self-pickup on campus, which saves delivery fees and allows direct handover from senior students to new students.

Currently, students mostly use open platforms like Mudah.my, Facebook Marketplace, and lifestyle social media apps like Xiaohongshu (RED) to buy and sell used items. For example, on Xiaohongshu or Facebook, a student simply posts a picture of the item and waits for interested buyers to send a private message. However, these platforms have problems with safety and searching. Since they are designed for general public use or social sharing, there is no proper system to filter campus-specific items. A post about selling a "study desk" can easily get lost in the feed algorithm or pushed down by other lifestyle posts, creating an "information overload" that makes it hard for users to search for specific items (Liu et al., 2024). More importantly, these open platforms do not verify if the user is a real student. Without a student email (.edu.my) verification system, students can easily be cheated by scammers or face personal safety risks during meetups. Therefore, a secure campus platform is needed to provide convenience and safety, while also supporting the Sustainable Development Goal 12 to reduce waste (​United Nations, 2025).

Review of Existing Trading Platforms
Currently, university students buy and sell second-hand items through different online platforms. As outlined previously in Chapter 1 (Table 1.1), these platforms can be divided into public e-commerce apps (like Mudah.my), social media (like Facebook and Xiaohongshu), and messaging groups (like Telegram and WhatsApp). While these platforms are easy to use, they are built for the general public, not for a closed campus environment. They fail to solve specific student problems, such as high delivery fees, messy information, and campus safety.
Analysis of Public Platforms 
Platforms like Mudah.my and Carousell are professional online marketplaces. They provide good features like clear categories and search filters, which allow users to find items easily. 
However, their biggest problem is the target audience. Because they are open to the general public, a student selling a used chair might attract buyers from another state. This makes face-to-face meetups impossible, and the shipping fee will be too expensive for a student to afford.
Furthermore, as shown in Table 1.1, these public platforms do not have special filters for university needs. For example, it is impossible to filter items by a specific university course or find semester-specific textbooks. They are designed for general trading, not for students.

Informal Campus Trading Channels
Because public platforms are troublesome for heavy items, many students turn to informal channels. They use social media (Facebook Marketplace, Xiaohongshu) or messaging apps (Telegram, WhatsApp) to trade. These platforms are very fast because a student can simply snap a picture, send it to a group or post it, and wait for a private message.
However, the discovery logic on these platforms is very poor (refer to Table 1.1). On Facebook or Xiaohongshu, the system uses feed algorithms. A post about a "study desk" will quickly be pushed down by other lifestyle posts or memes. After a few hours, it is very hard for buyers to find that specific post again.
Similarly, Telegram and WhatsApp rely on "chronological chat", meaning messages arranged by time. If someone posts a second-hand item in a university Telegram group, it will be buried as soon as other students start chatting or posting new items. Students have to scroll through hundreds of irrelevant text messages just to find one item, which causes high search costs and user frustration.
Limitation and Gap Analysis
The most serious weakness across all these existing platforms is the lack of user safety and trust mechanisms. On Mudah.my, Facebook, or Telegram, anyone can easily create a fake account. There is no way to check if the buyer or seller is actually a real student from the same university.
This "stranger-to-stranger" model leads to many scam cases. According to the Bukit Aman Commercial Crime Investigation Department (CCID), online scams and e-commerce fraud are among the highest reported cybercrimes in Malaysia, resulting in billions of Ringgit in losses as scammers often disappear after receiving payments (ZACK, 2025). Students are easy targets because they always look for cheap deals.
To solve this, the proposed Smart Platform uses a secure "peer-to-peer" model. As highlighted in Table 1.1, by forcing users to verify using their official university email (.edu.my), the platform makes sure every user is a real student. This removes outside scammers and makes physical meet-ups at designated campus zones much safer. This level of security is something that current public platforms and informal groups simply cannot provide.

Machine Learning in Recommender Systems
The integration of Machine Learning within the Smart Campus Second-Hand Trading Platform serves as a critical technical solution to elevate the user experience. Currently, mature public platforms like Mudah.my already offer highly detailed manual filtering systems. Users can navigate through subcategories, select specific brands, and sort items by price or upload time. However, this approach relies on a reactive "search-and-filter" model. It requires the user to actively input their parameters, apply multiple filters, and manually scroll through the sorted results every single time they open the application.
A recommender system solves this by shifting the paradigm from manual searching to proactive discovery. Instead of relying on the user to constantly tweak filters, the ML system automatically calculates the match between the user's profile and the available items. It then pushes a highly personalized feed of relevant products directly to the user's home screen.  This means a software engineering student looking for tech gadgets will automatically see a different home page compared to an architecture student looking for drawing tools. By intelligently filtering irrelevant data, this level of automated personalization solves the information overload problem and has been proven to significantly reduce the user's manual search time (Putra et al., 2025).
Content-based Filtering and Tag Extraction
Content-based Filtering (CBF) is the core machine learning concept selected for this project. The main idea is to recommend products based on the item's actual content rather than user popularity. However, a computer cannot visually "understand" a physical item like a human does. Therefore, the system must use a "tag-based" approach to represent the content digitally. In this system, every uploaded listing is converted into a set of text tags including specific categories, condition levels, and keywords from the description.
This means tag-based matching is simply the practical way the system implements Content-based Filtering. By matching these item tags with a stored User Profile, the system ensures that a student looking for a "gaming monitor" is presented with relevant listings immediately. This approach allows the application to overcome the "System Cold-Start" problem. It successfully connects buyers and sellers based purely on item attributes, even when the system is newly launched and lacks historical data (Chia & Najafabadi, 2022).

Figure 2.7: Conceptual Process of Tag Extraction and Profile Matching
As depicted in Figure 2.7, the Content-based Filtering process begins with the raw item listing uploaded by the seller. The system's Extraction Engine first analyzes unstructured data, including the item title and its text description. It then converts this information into a structured Item Vector consisting of standardized tags, such as specific categories and keywords. Simultaneously, the system maintains a User Vector based on the student's academic profile and search history. The recommendation engine then compares these two vectors to calculate their similarity. A match is identified when the engine detects a significant overlap in tags. For instance, if both the item vector and the user profile contain the keyword for a mechanical keyboard, the system will proactively recommend this specific item to the student.
Cosine Similarity as the Matching Engine
To make Content-based Filtering work mathematically, the system needs a formula to compare the user's preference tags with the item's tags. One of the most robust methods for this calculation is Cosine Similarity. This algorithm acts as the matching engine that turns text tags into numerical vectors in a multi-dimensional space. The relationship is expressed through the following equation:
Cosine Similarity = A  BA B
In a practical scenario, vector A represents the tags a student is searching for and vector B represents the tags of an uploaded item. If both vectors point to similar concepts, the angle between them is close to zero. This results in a similarity score closer to 1 which indicates a high match. Conversely, if the vectors represent completely different items, the score drops to 0. Using Cosine Similarity provides a high degree of accuracy for text and tag matching while operating with reduced computational complexity, making it highly efficient for the system (Permana & Wibowo, 2023).

Figure 2.8: Mathematical Representation of Cosine Similarity for Item Recommendation
As illustrated in Figure 2.8, the recommendation engine maps both user profiles and item attributes into a multi-dimensional vector space. In this simplified 2D model, the axes represent specific tag categories, such as "Tech Tags" and "Study Purpose Tags." Vector A represents the profile of an Information Technology student looking for study-related electronics. Vector B represents an uploaded item, such as a used monitor. Because the monitor shares similar tags with the student's profile, the angle between the two vectors is extremely small. This results in a Cosine Similarity score close to 1, prompting the system to push this item to the user's feed.
Conversely, Vector C represents an item with entirely different tags, such as a drawing canvas. While it holds academic value, it lacks the tech-related attributes required by the IT student. Consequently, the angle between Vector A and Vector C is large (approximately 90 degrees). This generates a Cosine Similarity score near 0, meaning the system will automatically filter it out. This visual model demonstrates how the algorithm proactively matches relevance without requiring users to manually configure search filters.
Rationalization for Selecting Content-based Filtering
When designing a recommender system, developers typically evaluate two primary techniques known as Collaborative Filtering and Content-based Filtering. Collaborative Filtering is the industry standard used by major platforms like Netflix or Shopee. It works by analyzing user behavior patterns to suggest items based on what similar users have bought in the past. This logic relies heavily on massive historical data and assumes that if User A and User B bought the same phone, they will like the same accessories.
However, this collaborative approach is fundamentally flawed for a campus second-hand market. The primary reason is that almost all second-hand items are unique one-off listings. Once a specific used study desk is sold, it is permanently removed from the database so the system cannot build a purchase history for it. Furthermore, Collaborative Filtering suffers from the "Cold Start" problem as it requires thousands of transactions before it can make accurate recommendations.
Therefore, this project selects Content-based Filtering as the core recommendation logic. In contrast to the collaborative method, Content-based Filtering does not rely on the actions of other users or historical sales data. It focuses strictly on the attributes and tags of the item itself. Even if a second-hand item is uploaded for the very first time, the system can immediately recommend it to a student who has those specific tags in their profile. This independent functioning ensures that the discovery logic remains fully reliable for unique items, proving it to be the most suitable technical choice for a specialized marketplace environment (Portugal et al., 2018).
Evaluation of Carbon Footprint Modeling for Sustainability
The integration of a sustainability tracking feature within the Smart Campus Second-Hand Trading Platform aligns with the global shift towards the Circular Economy. It directly supports the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 12, which emphasizes responsible consumption and production (​United Nations, 2025). Unlike traditional e-commerce, second-hand trading directly contributes to environmental preservation. It extends the lifecycle of products and prevents functional items from entering landfills prematurely. To quantify this contribution, the system utilizes a Carbon Footprint Modeling approach to calculate the estimated "Carbon Savings" generated by each successful campus transaction.
Methodology for Calculating Carbon Savings
Estimating the carbon footprint of second-hand goods is primarily based on the concept of Avoided Emissions in Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) studies. The environmental benefit of a second-hand purchase is calculated by estimating the emissions of a new product that was never manufactured because the used item was bought instead. For example, trading a used laptop on campus avoids the high carbon cost of raw material extraction, factory manufacturing, and international shipping (André et al., 2019).
Due to the computational complexity and data-intensive nature of performing a full-scale LCA for every unique item, the proposed system adopts a granular, sub-category estimation model. Rather than assigning a single arbitrary value to a broad category like "Electronics", the system maps specific item types to average Carbon Emission Factors (CEF). These average values are derived from established industry reports and environmental databases, such as manufacturer sustainability publications.
For instance, the database structure will assign an estimated carbon savings of 155 kg CO2e to a "Laptop" sub-category. When a transaction is completed, the system automatically retrieves the CEF associated with that specific item type and adds it to the user's total record. This tiered approach ensures reasonable accuracy for the sustainability dashboard without overloading the system's database architecture.
To ensure these estimated values are grounded in reality, the system references official Life Cycle Assessments from major electronics manufacturers. For example, as illustrated in Figure 2.9, Apple's environmental report for the MacBook Air 15-inch (M4 chip) indicates a total product carbon emission of 155 kg. According to the report, this figure is derived after accounting for significant emissions reductions from a 292 kg baseline, achieved through the manufacturer's use of recycled materials such as 100% recycled aluminum and renewable electricity. By adopting such specific, industry-standard baselines, the platform's Carbon Savings metrics remain scientifically justifiable and relevant to the actual tech products traded on campus.

The Role of Hyper-Local Logistics in Emission Reduction
A significant factor in the sustainability of the proposed platform is the reduction of last-mile delivery emissions. Traditional e-commerce logistics involve courier vans, motorbikes, and heavy plastic packaging. These elements contribute substantially to the carbon footprint of small consumer goods (Buldeo Rai et al., 2022).
In contrast, the campus-based platform operates on a hyper-local peer-to-peer model. It encourages physical meet-ups and self-pickups at designated safe zones. These exchanges usually occur on foot or via existing campus shuttle buses. This hyper-local exchange completely eliminates the transport-related carbon footprint compared to standard public platforms where items are shipped across states. Furthermore, physical campus meet-ups remove the need for single-use plastic packaging and bubble wrap, further reducing environmental waste.
Promoting Environmental Awareness through Data Visualization
Beyond technical calculation, the implementation of carbon modeling serves an important educational purpose. The platform transforms abstract environmental goals into tangible numbers by presenting users with a Personalized Sustainability Dashboard. Users can clearly see metrics such as their "Total Kilograms of CO2 Saved" displayed on their profile.
Studies on user engagement in green technologies suggest that providing immediate and data-driven feedback significantly increases user loyalty. Gamifying these metrics encourages long-term participation in sustainable behaviors (Douglas & Brauer, 2021). This feature distinguishes the Smart Campus Platform from informal messaging groups like WhatsApp. It also provides the university administration with valuable structured data to report on the institution's overall progress toward national green campus initiatives.
Review of Development Technologies
The selection of an appropriate development stack is fundamental to ensuring the platform's performance and long-term maintainability. This section evaluates the front-end and back-end technologies considered for the Smart Campus Second-Hand Trading Platform.
Front-End Framework: Flutter vs. Native Development
Flutter is an open-source UI software development kit created by Google. It enables developers to build applications for mobile, web, and desktop from a single codebase using the Dart programming language. Its primary strength lies in its custom graphics engine called Skia. This provides a high degree of control over the user interface and ensures consistent visual performance across different operating systems (Flutter.dev, 2025).
In contrast, Native Development involves using platform-specific languages like Kotlin for Android and Swift for iOS. This approach allows direct access to hardware components and the latest operating system features. It often results in the highest possible execution speed. Back-End Environment: Node.js vs. Traditional Servers
For the back-end infrastructure, Node.js is evaluated against traditional thread-based environments like Apache with PHP. Node.js is a JavaScript runtime built on Chrome's V8 engine. It utilizes an event-driven and non-blocking I/O model. This architecture allows it to handle numerous concurrent connections efficiently without consuming large amounts of memory (Tilkov & Vinoski, 2010).
On the other hand, traditional servers typically follow a multi-threaded model. In this model, each incoming request creates a new thread. While this is effective for heavy CPU calculations, it can lead to significant memory consumption when dealing with a high volume of simultaneous users. The project adopts Node.js as the back-end environment due to its superior efficiency in handling asynchronous tasks. These tasks include real-time messaging and concurrent image uploads for second-hand items. Chapter Summary and Evaluation
This chapter has provided a comprehensive review of the theoretical and technical foundations required to develop the Smart Campus Second-Hand Trading Platform. It began with a situational analysis of the current campus environment. The shift toward digital learning and the logistical challenges of student housing have created a highly fragmented secondary market. This review investigated the limitations of existing public platforms like Mudah.my and informal social media channels like Telegram. Through this analysis, it identified critical gaps in search efficiency, institutional trust, and transactional safety.
In terms of technical exploration, the review justified the use of Machine Learning to solve the problem of information overload. It selected Content-based Filtering to ensure that item discovery is personalized and accurate through tag-based matching. Furthermore, the inclusion of Carbon Footprint Modeling elevates the platform from a simple marketplace to a sustainability tool. This feature successfully quantifies the environmental benefits of the circular economy within the campus.
Finally, the evaluation of the development stack established Flutter and Node.js as the most viable technical frameworks. They will be utilized to deliver a high-performance, cross-platform solution with reliable back-end capabilities. Collectively, these findings provide a robust justification for the proposed system. The research confirms that the platform can effectively bridge the gap between technological innovation and the real-world needs of the university community.
